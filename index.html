Hospital use among adults prescribed antidepressants in NHS Tayside

Key finding

Younger adults prescribed antidepressants had the greatest relative excess in hospital use compared with the Scottish population rates for the same age.

Adults aged 16–24 prescribed antidepressants had 6.25 times the emergency department attendance rate, and 5.42 times the psychiatric admission rate, of Scottish adults of the same age. Adults aged 75 and over had the highest general acute admission rates, but little excess relative to their age peers.

Understand the comparison

The study reports two kinds of number. They answer different questions, and the headline finding comes from looking at both together.

Absolute rate

Hospital contacts per 1,000 adults prescribed antidepressants in the corresponding month

This tells you how much hospital use there was in a group. It was calculated separately for general acute admissions, emergency department attendances and psychiatric admissions.

Rate ratio

That rate divided by the corresponding Scottish population rate

This tells you how much more (or less) hospital use there was compared with Scottish adults of the same age group, or the same sex.

The calculation, step by step

  1. 1
    Count the hospital contacts among adults prescribed antidepressants in a given month, for one setting (for example, emergency department attendances).
  2. 2
    Divide by the number of adults prescribed antidepressants that month and multiply by 1,000. This is the absolute monthly rate: contacts per 1,000 adults prescribed antidepressants.
  3. 3
    Divide by the Scottish population rate for the same group (the same age band, or the same sex). This is the rate ratio. Monthly rate ratios were averaged over the 60 months from January 2019 to December 2023.

Why both numbers matter

The groups with the highest absolute rates were not the groups with the greatest excess relative to their peers.

Adults aged 75 and over

41.86 general acute admissions per 1,000 per month: the highest of any age group

0.92× the Scottish rate for this age group: no excess

Hospital use rises with age in everyone, so a high absolute rate here is largely what you would expect for this age.

Adults aged 16–24

12.31 general acute admissions per 1,000 per month: the lowest of any age group

1.80× the Scottish rate for this age group, rising to 6.25× for emergency attendances and 5.42× for psychiatric admissions

Young adults rarely need hospital care, so even modest absolute rates represent a large excess over their peers.

Where the Scottish population rates come from
  • Hospital activity for the Scottish population was obtained from Public Health Scotland.
  • Population denominators came from National Records of Scotland mid‑year population estimates.
  • Psychiatric admission counts were obtained directly from the Public Health Scotland Mental Health Analytical Team, because publicly available psychiatric activity data report discharges rather than admissions.
  • Age‑specific and sex‑specific Scottish rates were calculated and compared with the corresponding rates among adults prescribed antidepressants. The age and sex comparisons were made separately: the age comparison does not adjust for sex, and the sex comparison does not adjust for age.
  • No deprivation‑specific Scottish comparator was available, so rate ratios by deprivation quintile could not be calculated.
  • The Scottish comparison population includes people prescribed antidepressants, which is likely to make the observed differences smaller than a comparison with people not prescribed antidepressants would be.

How we did this study

A population‑based retrospective observational study using anonymised linked prescribing and hospital activity data for NHS Tayside, Scotland, from 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2023. Tap a step for more detail.

  1. 1Define the population, month by monthAdults aged 16 and over, resident in NHS Tayside, with at least one antidepressant prescription in that month

    For each calendar month from January 2019 to December 2023, the study population comprised adults (aged 16 years or over) resident in NHS Tayside who had at least one antidepressant prescription recorded during that month.

    Eligibility was reassessed every month, so the population changed as people started and stopped treatment. This gives 60 monthly snapshots rather than a fixed cohort followed over time. The same person can appear in many months.

    In an average month the population comprised 68,876 adults: 45,533 (66.1%) female and 23,343 (33.9%) male. The largest age group was 55–64 (19.3%). The monthly number increased by 8.3% between January 2019 and December 2023, with a transient dip when COVID‑19 restrictions were introduced in March 2020. Growth was greatest among adults aged 75 and over (+24.8%) and those living in the least deprived areas (+20.8%).

  2. 2Link prescribing and hospital recordsThree national hospital datasets linked to prescribing data

    Prescribing data were linked to three national administrative datasets held by Public Health Scotland:

    • SMR01, the Scottish Morbidity Record for general acute inpatient and day‑case activity: used to identify general acute admissions.
    • A&E2, the Accident and Emergency DataMart: used to identify emergency department attendances.
    • SMR04, the Scottish Morbidity Record for psychiatric inpatient activity: used to identify psychiatric admissions.

    Socioeconomic deprivation was measured with the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation (SIMD), an area‑based measure derived from postcode, grouped into quintiles from SIMD1 (most deprived) to SIMD5 (least deprived). SIMD was unavailable for 0.4% of the population where postcode data were missing.

    Comorbidity burden was assessed with the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (with depression excluded). Because Elixhauser data come from recorded hospital activity, comorbidity analyses were restricted to people with hospital contact.

  3. 3Calculate monthly hospital use ratesContacts per 1,000 adults prescribed antidepressants, for each setting, by age, sex and deprivation

    The primary outcomes were monthly rates of general acute admissions, emergency department attendances and psychiatric admissions. For each setting, hospital use was expressed as the number of contacts per 1,000 adults prescribed antidepressants in the corresponding month.

    Rates were examined over the five‑year period and stratified by:

    • Age: 16–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74 and 75 years or over.
    • Sex: male and female.
    • Deprivation: SIMD quintiles 1 (most deprived) to 5 (least deprived).

    Statistical tests. Differences in mean monthly rates between males and females were assessed with paired t‑tests. Differences across age groups and SIMD quintiles were assessed with one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Tukey post‑hoc tests for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses and data visualisation were performed in GraphPad Prism version 11.10.2.

  4. 4Compare with Scottish population ratesAge‑ and sex‑specific rate ratios

    To put hospital use in context, rates among adults prescribed antidepressants were compared with the corresponding rates in the wider Scottish population. Scottish hospital activity was obtained from Public Health Scotland, with population denominators from National Records of Scotland mid‑year estimates. Psychiatric admission counts were obtained directly from the Public Health Scotland Mental Health Analytical Team.

    Age‑specific and sex‑specific population rates were calculated and compared with the corresponding rates among people prescribed antidepressants. A rate ratio is the Tayside antidepressant‑prescribed rate divided by the Scottish rate: a value above 1 indicates higher use and a value below 1 indicates lower use. See Understand the comparison.

Analyses not shown on the poster

Reasons for hospital admission

Primary reasons for admission were identified from International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‑10) primary diagnosis codes. The most common admission diagnoses were identified for each study year to see how the diagnostic profile of hospital activity changed over time.

Common diagnoses comprised pneumonia (J12–J18), lower respiratory tract infection (J22), COPD exacerbation (J44.1), COVID‑19 (U07.1), chest pain unspecified (R07.4) and other chest pain (R07.3), urinary tract infection (N39.0) and abdominal pain (R10.4).

Selected outcomes previously associated with antidepressant use were also examined: falls (W19), neck of femur fracture (S72.0), syncope (R55), delirium (F05), hyponatraemia (E87.1), gastrointestinal bleeding (K92.0–K92.2), antidepressant poisoning (T43.0–T43.2) and paracetamol poisoning (T39.1). Annual frequencies were examined across the study period.

What we found. The most frequent primary diagnoses were acute medical conditions: respiratory infections, chest pain, urinary tract infections and abdominal pain. This profile was broadly stable across the five years, although respiratory admissions fell during the pandemic and COVID‑19 appeared as a distinct but transient diagnosis. Among the selected outcomes, falls and paracetamol poisoning were the most frequent reasons for admission. Falls and syncope increased over the period, whereas gastrointestinal bleeding and hyponatraemia remained relatively stable. See Figure 3.

Comorbidity (Elixhauser index)

Among adults with recorded hospital activity, the mean Elixhauser comorbidity count (excluding depression) was 1.51. Annual mean counts declined between 2019 and 2023 across general acute, emergency and psychiatric settings, with the largest reduction among those admitted to psychiatric care. Because the index is derived from hospital records, this decline may reflect changes in who was admitted or how diagnoses were recorded rather than a true reduction in multimorbidity.

Temporal and seasonal patterns

General acute admissions and emergency department attendances accounted for almost all recorded hospital use (48.4% and 49.0% of contacts respectively); psychiatric admissions accounted for 2.6%. General acute and emergency rates fell sharply after COVID‑19 restrictions were introduced in March 2020, recovered from late 2021, but remained below pre‑pandemic levels at the end of 2023. Psychiatric admission rates fluctuated month to month with no clear trend. Seasonal variation was limited: emergency attendances were modestly higher in summer than autumn (p = 0.01), with no clear seasonal pattern in the other settings. See Figure 2.

Interpretation & limitations

What the findings suggest, why they matter, and what they can and cannot tell us.

What the findings suggest

  • Absolute burden and relative excess point to different groups. Older adults had the highest general acute admission rates, but younger adults had the greatest excess compared with their age‑matched peers. In adults aged 75 and over, general acute and emergency rates were similar to, or slightly below, Scottish rates for that age.
  • The excess in young adults was concentrated in emergency and psychiatric care. Adults aged 16–24 had roughly six times the emergency attendance rate and five times the psychiatric admission rate of their peers. This may reflect the onset or escalation of psychiatric illness in the transition to adulthood, when acute distress, self‑harm and substance misuse can precipitate emergency presentation, but these mechanisms could not be tested with aggregate data.
  • Men had higher use and a greater relative excess than women in every setting, most strikingly for psychiatric admissions (3.88 vs 2.77 times the Scottish rate). Lower help‑seeking and later presentation among men, higher chronic disease burden, and sex differences in how mental illness presents are plausible contributors, but remain hypotheses.
  • Hospital use rose steadily with area deprivation across all three settings, consistent with the established link between socioeconomic disadvantage and poorer mental and physical health.
  • Most admissions were for common acute medical conditions rather than outcomes specifically linked to antidepressants. Adults prescribed antidepressants are a clinically diverse group whose hospital needs extend well beyond psychiatric care.

Why it matters

Absolute rates show where demand is greatest; relative rates identify groups whose use exceeds what would be expected for their age or sex. The two have different implications for planning services. High absolute general acute admission rates in older adults point to medication review, falls prevention and coordinated management of multimorbidity. The large relative excess in younger adults suggests that routine antidepressant review in this group could extend beyond treatment response and side effects to include self‑harm and substance misuse risk, physical health, continuity of mental healthcare and barriers to planned support. The pattern in men raises the question of whether follow‑up pathways engage men effectively before needs escalate to crisis, and the deprivation gradient indicates that prescribing decisions alone are unlikely to address the wider drivers of hospital use.

What we can conclude

  • Hospital use among adults prescribed antidepressants in NHS Tayside varied substantially by age, sex and deprivation across general acute, emergency and psychiatric settings.
  • Relative to the Scottish population of the same age, the excess was greatest in younger adults and smallest, or absent, in the oldest.
  • Men prescribed antidepressants had higher absolute and relative hospital use than women.
  • Antidepressant prescribing identifies a population in whom underlying mental, physical and social needs may be concentrated.

What we cannot conclude

  • That antidepressants caused higher hospital use. The design is observational and descriptive. The excess may reflect the underlying illness being treated, chronic pain, physical multimorbidity, deprivation and other differences between the populations.
  • That an individual on antidepressants is more likely to be admitted. Rates count contacts, not people: a higher rate could mean more people using hospitals, repeated contacts by a smaller group, or both.
  • How much of the excess would remain after adjustment. The data could not adjust for indication, severity, antidepressant type or dose, treatment duration, other medicines or individual comorbidity, and age and sex were compared separately rather than together.
  • That the comparison is with people not taking antidepressants. The Scottish population includes people prescribed antidepressants, which is likely to make the differences appear smaller than they would be against an untreated group.
Strengths and limitations in more detail

Strengths. The study used linked, routinely collected prescribing and hospital data with population‑wide coverage across NHS Tayside. Five years of monthly rates span the period before, during and after the acute disruption of the COVID‑19 pandemic. Three national hospital datasets captured general acute, emergency and psychiatric care within one framework. Monthly denominators reflected the population actually prescribed antidepressants in each month, and stratification revealed heterogeneity that an overall rate would hide. Benchmarking against population rates identified groups whose hospital use was disproportionately high for their age or sex.

Limitations.

  • The observational, descriptive design precludes causal inference. Results should not be read as showing that antidepressants caused hospitalisation.
  • No adjustment was possible for prescribing indication, severity of mental illness, antidepressant class or dose, treatment duration, adherence, concomitant medicines or individual comorbidity.
  • Analyses used aggregated monthly rates rather than following individuals. The same person can appear in many months and have several contacts, so monthly observations are not fully independent and the statistical certainty of group differences may be overstated.
  • Elixhauser comorbidity counts were available only for people with hospital contact and depend on how diagnoses were coded. The decline over time may reflect changes in who was admitted or in recording rather than less multimorbidity.
  • The comparison populations are not geographically equivalent (Tayside vs all of Scotland), and the Scottish comparator includes people prescribed antidepressants. Age and sex were compared separately, without simultaneous adjustment for age, sex, deprivation and comorbidity. No deprivation‑specific Scottish comparator was available.
  • Selected antidepressant‑associated outcomes were identified from primary diagnosis codes only; admissions where they were recorded as secondary diagnoses would be missed.
  • SIMD is an area‑level measure and may not reflect individual circumstances.
  • The COVID‑19 pandemic disrupted service access, admission thresholds, health‑seeking behaviour and capacity, so changes between 2020 and 2023 cannot be attributed solely to changes in clinical need.
How this compares with other studies
  • The 8.3% rise in the prescribed population is consistent with sustained global growth in antidepressant use over the past decade. The predominance of women matches European prescribing patterns and the higher recorded prevalence of depression among women.
  • The largest relative growth in prescribing was in adults aged 75 and over, even though this group showed no excess hospital use relative to their peers. Growth in the treated population should not be read as evidence of rising morbidity or excess hospital use in that group.
  • Earlier studies of older adults reported increased admissions among antidepressant users, but they compared exposed with unexposed older adults, or treatment phases within a population already experiencing polypharmacy. Here the comparator was the whole age‑matched Scottish population, so near‑unity rate ratios in the oldest group indicate little excess relative to an already high baseline, not low need.
  • The sixfold excess in emergency attendance and fivefold excess in psychiatric admission at ages 16–24 accords with Scotland‑wide evidence that children and adolescents receiving antidepressants had twice the risk of all‑cause hospitalisation of their peers, and extends that pattern into early adulthood.
  • Paracetamol poisoning was more frequent than antidepressant poisoning, suggesting that hospital use in this population may reflect underlying self‑harm risk and wider psychosocial vulnerability rather than direct antidepressant toxicity alone.
What should happen next
  • Individual patient‑level studies with a comparison group of adults not prescribed antidepressants, to test whether the excess persists after adjustment for indication, illness severity, comorbidity and deprivation.
  • Longitudinal follow‑up to see how hospital use changes after antidepressants are started, changed or stopped.
  • Analyses of first and repeat contacts, the diagnoses attached to each, and transitions between general acute, emergency and psychiatric care, to learn whether a small group of frequent users drives the aggregate pattern.
  • Qualitative work with younger adults and men prescribed antidepressants to identify barriers to preventive care and the routes that lead to crisis or emergency presentation.
  • Using the age, sex and deprivation patterns described here to guide service planning, and prospectively evaluating more integrated follow‑up in the groups with the highest use.
